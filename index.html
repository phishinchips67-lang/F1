Section 01 — Architecture

Not Two Engines.
One Integrated System.

The SF-26 doesn't have two separate engines running in parallel. It has one power unit — five physical components that share a single crankshaft and are coordinated every millisecond by Ferrari's control electronics. Hover each block below to see what it does.

Section 04 — Physics

How the
MGU-K Works

It's a Permanent Magnet Synchronous Machine (PMSM) — same fundamental principle as the servo drives and VFD motors in industrial automation, operating at extreme power density and 50,000 RPM. One machine. Two modes. The inverter decides which direction energy flows.

Crankshaft gets extra torque

In motor mode, the SiC inverter converts battery DC into 3-phase AC. That AC creates a rotating magnetic field in the stator. The rotor's permanent magnets chase that field — generating torque on the crankshaft. At corner exit, this happens instantly (zero lag), while the ICE is still building revs.

The control architecture runs as nested real-time loops — the same layered control logic used in industrial servo drives, just at ten times the power:

ms
Outer Loop — Strategy
Lap-level energy budget. How many MJ to target per sector. Updated by Ferrari's pit wall. Pre-computed per circuit.
µs
Middle Loop — Torque Demand
Reads Lewis's throttle/brake inputs. Translates to a torque request blended between ICE and MGU-K in real time.
ns
Inner Loop — FOC Current Control
Field Oriented Control. Decomposes stator current into flux + torque components. SiC MOSFETs switch at tens of kHz. Exact torque delivery.
Section 05 — Circuit Strategy

The Battery Is Never
The Same Twice

How much energy Lewis can recover per lap depends entirely on the circuit. A track with heavy braking is an energy-rich environment. A flat-out track like Monza is an energy desert. The FIA adjusts the recovery cap per race to prevent artificial driving.
